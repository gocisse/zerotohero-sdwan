**By the end of this lesson you will be able to:**

- tell the <Term id="underlay" /> from the <Term id="overlay" /> and say what belongs in each;
- explain what carries the tunnels and what protects them;
- describe what <Term id="transport-independence" /> lets you do when circuits change;
- name three things the underlay still affects, even though the overlay hides most of it.

## Two layers, one network

Section 1 introduced the two words. Here we make them precise.

- The **underlay** is the set of circuits and carrier networks that physically move packets: an Internet line, an MPLS circuit, a cellular
  connection, the routers and NAT devices along the way. The underlay is where the carriers' responsibility ends.
- The **overlay** is what the VeloCloud components build on top: <Term id="tunnel">tunnels</Term> between Edges and Gateways, and directly between Edges, plus
  the routing and policy that use them.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Course vocabulary">
  "Underlay" is our word. Arista's guides describe the same layers in other terms, for example "WAN links", "transports" and "the SD-WAN
  overlay", and they use "overlay" in names such as <Term id="overlay-flow-control">Overlay Flow Control</Term>. Use whichever term your own documentation uses; the layers are
  the same.
</Callout>

## What carries the tunnels

The overlay tunnels run on **UDP port 2426**: <Term id="vcmp" /> is registered with IANA on that port, and it is used for tunnel management and information
exchange between two tunnel endpoints (Edge and Gateway, or Edge and Edge). Arista's guide says VCMP is encrypted with transport-mode
<Term id="ipsec" />, and that VeloCloud forces <Term id="nat-traversal">NAT traversal</Term> on with the special port 2426, so its behaviour is the same whether or not a
NAT device is present on the path. The next two lessons use these facts.

## What lives in each layer

| | Underlay | Overlay |
|---|---|---|
| Made of | circuits, carrier networks, routers, NAT devices, firewalls | tunnels, tunnel endpoints (Edges, Gateways), routes exchanged between them |
| Owned by | carriers and the site's own network team | the SD-WAN (configured from the Orchestrator) |
| Measured by | the Edge, per path and per link | the same: DMPO measures loss, latency and jitter on every path |
| Policy talks about | nothing: it only moves packets | applications, transport groups, WAN links, interfaces |

Arista's Business Policy page describes **<Term id="transport-group">Transport Group</Term>** <Term id="link-steering">steering</Term> as an abstraction of the actual interfaces, so one policy can apply across
different device types or locations. That abstraction is what makes transport independence practical.

## Transport independence in practice

What does it buy you? Look at the last three steps of the diagram.

- **Add a circuit.** A site adds a cellular connection. The applications and the data center do not change; the Edge has one more circuit to
  carry a tunnel. Arista's overview lists private line, cable, DSL, 4G or 5G and satellite as links an Edge can aggregate.
- **Remove a circuit.** One disappears. The tunnels over the others keep working, and DMPO detects an SLA failure or outage within 300 to 500
  milliseconds and steers flows to a healthy link where one exists.
- **Describe intent, not cables.** Policy can refer to groups of circuits (transport groups) instead of to a physical port, so the same
  policy fits a site with MPLS and broadband and a site with broadband and LTE.

<Predict

  answer={0}
>
  Transport independence means policy talks about applications and groups of transports, not about a specific cable or carrier. The
  circuit, the port it uses and any NAT in front of it are underlay details that change.
</Predict>

## Independence is not indifference

The overlay hides *most* of the underlay, not all of it. Three examples the guides make explicit:

1. **<Term id="mtu">MTU</Term>.** Tunnels add headers (lesson 7), so the underlay's packet size limit bounds the overlay's. <Term id="path-mtu-discovery">Path MTU discovery</Term> even behaves
   differently by underlay type: on public Internet links discovery runs toward the Gateways, and on private WAN links toward the other Edges.
2. **NAT and firewalls.** A NAT device in front of an Edge can stop other Edges from building a direct tunnel to it (lesson 5), and firewalls
   have to allow the tunnel ports (lesson 6).
3. **Capacity.** After detecting a WAN link, the Edge runs a bandwidth test against the nearest Gateway to learn what the link can actually carry.

<Callout variant="gotcha" title="'Transport independent' does not mean 'transport blind'">
  If a tunnel will not come up over one circuit and works over another, look in the underlay first: a firewall rule, a NAT device or a
  size limit that one carrier handles differently from the other is a
  place to check before assuming an SD-WAN fault.
</Callout>

## Key takeaways

- Underlay = the circuits; overlay = the tunnels (VCMP on UDP 2426, protected with IPsec) and routing built on them.
- Policy and tunnels are designed to survive circuits being added, removed or swapped.
- The underlay still shapes MTU, NAT behaviour, firewall needs and capacity.

## What would you look for in the VCO

- **Monitor > Edges > (an Edge) > Links** lists the WAN links with their measurements, including a transport-group view. These are the underlay circuits as the Edge sees them.
- **Monitor > Edges > (an Edge) > Paths** lists the paths to the Edge's SD-WAN peers with quality scores. These are the overlay paths.
- In the Edge's device settings, the **WAN overlay** option on an interface says whether that interface carries overlay tunnels.
- In a business-policy rule, the **Action** tab's link steering offers **Transport Group**, **Interface** and **WAN Link**: three ways of naming the underlay in policy.
