- describe what <Term id="broadband" /> adds, and what it lacks.

## What a WAN is for

A company rarely fits in one building. There are offices, shops and factories (we will call them <Term id="branch">branches</Term>) and
there are places where the company's applications actually run, usually one or more <Term id="data-center">data centers</Term>. The
network that joins them over long distances is the <Term id="wan" />.

For a long time the WAN had one dominant job: carry traffic from branches to applications in the data center. Almost every design
decision you will meet in this lesson follows from that single assumption, and the assumption stopped being true.

## The classic design: private circuits and a hub

In the traditional design every branch buys a **private circuit** from a carrier, and all of those circuits meet at the headquarters
data center. That layout is called <Term id="hub-and-spoke" />: the data center is the hub, the branches are the spokes. The carrier's
private service is commonly <Term id="mpls" />, and in this course "MPLS" mostly means "the private circuits".

Arista's own overview of the problem is a fair summary of the trade-off. MPLS typically gives **high-quality service**, but with
**limited capacity, higher cost and long lead times** to get a new circuit installed. Step through the diagram below and watch what
happens to two kinds of traffic.

## Backhauling, and why the cloud broke the model

When applications lived in the data center, sending everything to the data center was exactly right. Then the applications moved.
Collaboration tools, email and business software increasingly run as <Term id="saas" /> in the provider's cloud, and branch users
consume more and more bandwidth doing so. The data center is no longer where the traffic wants to go, yet the WAN still sends it there.

Arista's overview describes the consequence for branches that only have private circuits: they rely on backhauling all cloud, SaaS
and Internet traffic through the enterprise data center, which **adds latency, degrades application performance and drives up
bandwidth costs**. It also notes that a traditional hub-and-spoke architecture might not support a move to the cloud.

In this simplified design, two things get worse together:

- **Distance.** Every request and reply travels to the data center and back before it even starts the journey to the provider.
- **Concentration.** All branches' cloud traffic converges on one Internet link and one firewall at the hub, which must be sized for
  everyone.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Backhauling is not always a mistake">
  Some organizations backhaul on purpose, for example so that all Internet traffic passes through one security inspection point.
  The problem is not that backhauling exists; it is that it is the *only* option when the branch has no alternative path.
</Callout>

## Broadband: fast and cheap, but not assured

The obvious fix is to give each branch an ordinary Internet connection: <Term id="broadband" />, such as DSL, cable or LTE. Arista's
overview credits broadband with fast deployment and greater capacity, and warns that it can lack **reliability, security and assured
performance**, which means a poor experience for the user. Branches are served by private circuits, by Internet connections, or
by both, and, as the overview puts it, neither is ideal on its own.

Letting a branch send Internet-bound traffic straight out is called <Term id="dia" />, and the next lesson shows how that changes the
picture. But notice the new problem it creates: two kinds of circuit with different strengths, and something has to decide, per
application, which one to use.

## The management problem

Circuits are only half the story. Arista's overview also points out that traditional branch networks tend to lack **centralized
management, control and visibility**, and that having many different management tools makes it harder to troubleshoot or to respond
to threats. Compliance rules such as PCI, HIPAA and GDPR are harder to keep up across many branches when each has different security
products from different vendors.

## Key takeaways

- The traditional WAN was built to carry branch traffic to applications in a data center.
- MPLS is typically high quality but limited in capacity, costly, and slow to deploy.
- When applications moved to SaaS, backhauling through the data center added delay and cost.
- Broadband is quick and plentiful but not assured, so networks end up with a mix, and a new question: which traffic uses which link?
