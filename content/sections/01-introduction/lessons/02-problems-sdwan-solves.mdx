**By the end of this lesson you will be able to:**

- name the five problems SD-WAN is meant to address and explain each in one sentence;
- say what <Term id="sd-wan" /> means in plain language;
- describe how <Term id="zero-touch-provisioning" /> and the two Edge activation methods work;
- explain why a central <Term id="business-policy" /> matters once a branch has more than one kind of link.

## Five problems, one idea

Lesson 1 left us with a list of frustrations. Here they are grouped the way a business would raise them. The right-hand column is the
**vendor's view** of how SD-WAN responds, taken from Arista's own overview.

| Problem | What hurts today | What SD-WAN promises |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Leased lines are cost-prohibitive and slow to deploy for the bandwidth branches need | Lower cost of ownership, by using Internet circuits alongside private ones (vendor's claim) |
| Agility | Architectural complexity makes branch changes slow | One central place to define how the network behaves |
| Visibility | Many tools, no single view, hard to troubleshoot | A single management portal with network-wide monitoring |
| Branch rollout | New circuits have long lead times and sites need hands-on setup | Ship a device, plug it in, finish setup from the cloud |
| Transport choice | Neither private circuits nor Internet connections are ideal on their own | Use whatever links a site has and choose per application |

The common idea behind all five: **separate "what I want" from "which cable carries it"**. That separation is what the
<Term id="sd-wan" /> name points at. You describe intent once, centrally, and software works out how to use the circuits at each site.

## What "SD-WAN" means here

Put simply, an <Term id="sd-wan" /> adds a layer of software over the WAN circuits. Arista describes the goal as improving the economics
and flexibility of a hybrid WAN (one with both private and Internet circuits), with the deployment speed and low maintenance of a
cloud-based service, and with policy-based, network-wide control over application performance and visibility.

Two ideas to keep:

1. **Central policy.** Rules about applications and links are written once and applied to many sites. Arista says <Term id="link-steering">link steering</Term>, link
   <Term id="on-demand-remediation">remediation</Term> and <Term id="qos">QoS</Term> are applied automatically from the business policy, with room for site-specific overrides.
2. **Many links, one network.** An Edge can aggregate multiple links (Arista's overview lists private line, cable, DSL, 4G or 5G and
   satellite) and steer traffic over the best ones. We call the property of not caring which kind of link it is
   <Term id="transport-independence" />.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Course vocabulary">
  "Transport independence" and "underlay" are our own teaching words for ideas that Arista's guides describe in other terms. If you
  search the documentation for them you may not find them. The ideas are real; the labels are ours.
</Callout>

## Rolling out a branch without an engineer

The branch-rollout row deserves a closer look because it is one of the things you can see directly in the VCO. Arista's overview
describes <Term id="zero-touch-provisioning" />: the Edge is shipped to the branch, a person with no networking skills plugs in power
and a network cable, and activation, configuration and ongoing management are handled from the cloud.

The Orchestrator documentation describes two ways an Edge can be **activated**:

- **Push (auto-activation).** The Edge is powered on and connected to the Internet and activates itself. The branch only needs
  outbound Internet connectivity through DHCP. No activation link is involved, because the Edge is identified by its serial number and
  model, which an administrator has already assigned to a profile and a license in the Orchestrator.
- **Pull (activation email).** An administrator sends an activation email from the Orchestrator to a contact at the site. The contact
  connects the Edge and opens the link. The Edge then downloads its configuration and software from the Orchestrator and reboots
  several times to apply them.

<Callout variant="gotcha" title="Vendor claims are not your results">
  This lesson leans on Arista's own overview, which is a marketing-adjacent document. For example, it says the built-in firewall lets
  customers eliminate legacy branch firewalls without sacrificing security. That is the vendor's claim. A real design review would test
  it against your requirements. Throughout this course, we mark what is claimed versus what is documented behavior.
</Callout>

## Key takeaways

- The five problems: cost, agility, visibility, branch rollout, and transport choice.
- SD-WAN separates intent (central policy) from transport (whichever links a site has).
- Zero-touch means a non-expert plugs the Edge in; activation is either pushed (auto) or pulled (email).
- In the VCO, Profiles and Edge activation are where the rollout story becomes visible.

## What would you look for in the VCO

- **Configure > Profiles.** A **Profile** is a template configuration that you define once and apply to many Edges. The list shows
  how many Edges use each Profile (the *Used By* column). After installation a default **<Term id="quick-start-profile">Quick Start Profile</Term>** exists.
- **Configure > Edges.** The list of Edges. From an Edge's Overview you can send the activation email described above.
- **Service Settings > Edge Auto-activation.** The inventory of Edges waiting to auto-activate (*Available Inventory*) and those already
  assigned (*Assigned Inventory*).
- **Monitor > Network Overview.** A summary of activated Edges, links and the busiest applications: the "visibility" row of the table.
